# Jennifer extract call — prep brief

*GTM Lane VII.A — case study substrate. Two extraction targets in one call: the switching-narrative quote (for marketing/positioning) and pre/post-Dispatch metrics (for case study + investor narrative). Authored Day 53 chase #5.*

---

## Why this call is load-bearing

Jennifer is the only operator who has actually swapped from PMS-bundled housekeeping (ResNexus) to Dispatch. That makes her quote worth more than any feature comparison and her metrics the only real-world evidence Dispatch has at beta-stage. Three downstream surfaces depend on what comes out of this call:

1. **Marketing site hero copy** (Lane IV) — the quote sits above the fold on the homepage.
2. **Outbound trigger language** (Lane III) — her specific words inform the cold-email subject lines and opening hooks.
3. **Investor narrative** (Lane VIII) — the quantified delta becomes the traction slide.

Goal: extract her authentic operator voice (not her co-founder marketing voice), then approve-back-for-publication.

---

## The dual-voice problem (and how to solve it)

Jennifer has two voices: **operator** (specific, gritty, named pain) and **co-founder** (helpful, marketing-flavored, abstract). She'll default to the co-founder voice when she knows it's for marketing — that's worse content. The interview structure pulls her back into operator voice.

**Operator voice example:**
> "I was printing PDFs every morning and using a Sharpie on the whiteboard to track who was in which hall."

**Co-founder voice example:**
> "Dispatch is so much better because the user experience is more intuitive."

Use the operator quote. Discard the co-founder quote. The interview structure below biases hard toward the operator voice.

## Pre-call setup (10 min)

1. **Schedule for a low-stress hour** — not during a turnover surge, not 15 minutes before a shift change. Aim for 90 minutes blocked even though the call is 60 (gives room for tangent).
2. **Ask permission to record audio.** "I'm going to record so I can listen back and capture your exact phrasing — anything you say can be edited or struck before we use it." She'll say yes. Otter, Granola, Zoom recording, or phone voice memo all work.
3. **Have these notes open in front of you:**
   - This brief
   - `docs/gtm/lanes/01-positioning.md` (so the ResNexus narrative is fresh)
   - `docs/gtm/lanes/02-icp-and-pricing.md` (so the four mutation dimensions are at hand)
4. **Frame the call for her:** "I want to capture your story in your own words so we can use it on the marketing site and case study. Two things: (1) the story of why you built Dispatch instead of just using what was already there, and (2) some rough numbers on what changed. None of it goes anywhere until you approve every word."

## Call structure (60 min total)

### Phase 1 — Warm-up + story (15 min)

Goal: get her narrating, not answering testimonial questions. Open-ended only.

**Opening prompts (pick one or two; let her run):**
- "Walk me through what a typical Tuesday morning looked like before Dispatch. Start the moment you got to the property."
- "Tell me about the worst housekeeping morning you can remember from last summer."
- "When did you first realize the PMS housekeeping module wasn't going to work for you?"

**Listen for:**
- Specific physical objects (printouts, whiteboard, sticky notes, walkie-talkies, paper checklists)
- Specific named pain (specific guest, specific room, specific moment)
- Specific numbers (how many printouts, how many housekeepers, how often)
- Specific reactions (frustration, embarrassment, callouts from guests)

**Don't:**
- Lead her with "and Dispatch fixed that, right?" — let her get there on her own.
- Interrupt to clarify — note the question for later, let her finish the story.
- Ask about features. Ask about her day.

### Phase 2 — Targeted quote extraction (20 min)

Now narrow in on the three quote-target zones. For each zone, ask her open question, then probe for specificity.

**Zone A — Why she built/commissioned Dispatch (the switching-narrative quote)**

Open: "If a friend of yours opened an inn next door and asked you what software to use, what would you tell them about why you chose to build something instead of buying what existed?"

Probes if needed:
- "What did the existing tools have wrong?"
- "What was the deal-breaker?"
- "What was the thing you couldn't get from [ResNexus / Little Hotelier / Cloudbeds]?"

**Zone B — The four mutation dimensions in her words**

Open: "Think about a day where everything changed — a guest extended, someone called in sick, a maintenance issue popped up. What did you do before Dispatch? What do you do now?"

Probes:
- "When [specific change] happens at 10am, what did the day look like before? What does it look like now?"
- "Where did housekeepers find out about [change]?"
- "What's the one minute or one task that you don't have to do anymore?"

**Zone C — Staff side (the high-turnover thesis)**

Open: "Walk me through training a new housekeeper before Dispatch vs. now. What's different?"

Probes:
- "How long did it used to take before a new hire could be productive without a shadow?"
- "How many new hires do you train per year roughly?"
- "What was the hardest part of the old training?"

### Phase 3 — Metrics (15 min)

Goal: get rough numbers for each metric, even if she has to estimate. Estimates are useable. "I don't track that" is fine — capture both her qualitative read AND her best estimate.

**Ask each as a paired before/after:**

| Metric | Before Dispatch | After Dispatch | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours to train a new housekeeper to "no longer needs shadowing" | __ hrs | __ hrs | |
| Housekeeper turnover (people / year out of how many on staff) | __ / __ | __ / __ | |
| Paper printouts per shift (count or rough cost) | __ | __ | |
| Manager time spent on housekeeping coordination per day | __ min | __ min | |
| "Communication-fail" incidents per week (rooms turned to wrong status, double-cleans, missed turn-downs) | __ | __ | |
| Average departure-room-clean time | __ min | __ min | |
| Guest complaints related to room readiness per month | __ | __ | |

**Phrasing tip:** lead with the question, then offer a range to anchor. "How long did it take to train a new hire — like, half a shift, a full day, three days, a week?" That works better than open "how long?"

### Phase 4 — Wrap (10 min)

- "Anything I didn't ask that you wish I had?"
- "If I quote you on [paraphrase the strongest line she said], are you good with that?"
- "I'll send you the proposed quotes for approval before any of this goes anywhere. Look for them in your inbox by [day] — quick yes/no/edit on each."

End the recording.

## Post-call action plan (within 48 hours of the call)

1. **Listen back to the recording** with a timestamp tracker. Mark every moment where she said something specific, named, vivid, or quotable. Aim for 10-15 candidate quotes.
2. **Narrow to 5 quotes** that cover three angles:
   - The lead hero quote (one sentence, marketing-site above-the-fold-grade)
   - 2 supporting operational quotes (the four mutation dimensions, the staff side)
   - 2 short pull-quotes (one-line punchy callouts for outbound subject lines)
3. **Format the metrics table** with her numbers + her qualitative caveats.
4. **Email her** with the candidate quotes + the metrics table. Subject: "Quotes for your approval — Dispatch case study." Body: brief explanation + each quote individually + an "approve / edit / strike" button per quote. Use plain English, not contract language.
5. **Wait for her approval** before publishing anywhere.
6. **Land the approved assets:**

## Specific quote targets — what to listen for

Best-case hero quote shapes (so you know it when you hear it):

- "I tried [X], it didn't work because [specific operational reason], so I [did Y]."
- "Before, my housekeepers [specific physical thing they did]. Now [specific contrast]."
- "The moment [specific change happens], the whole day used to [specific failure]. Dispatch [specific outcome]."
- "If I had to go back to [the previous tool], I'd [specific reaction]."

Worst-case quotes to ignore (these are co-founder voice):
- "Dispatch has a great user experience."
- "It's just so much more intuitive."
- "I love how easy it is."
- "The team really likes it."

The good quotes are specific, sensory, named. The bad quotes are abstract, generic, marketing-fluffy. If she gives you a marketing-fluffy line, push back with "can you give me an example?" — that pulls her back into operator voice.

---

## What to do if she's not feeling reflective

Sometimes the operator brain stays in operator mode and resists reflection. If 20 minutes in, you're getting "I dunno, things are better now" answers — switch tactics:

- **Ask about a specific room.** "Tell me about a turn that went badly last week. What happened?"
- **Ask about a specific person.** "Tell me about your newest housekeeper. How did onboarding go?"
- **Ask about a specific guest moment.** "Tell me about the last guest who made a special request. What did your team do?"

Specific anchors break the abstraction loop.

---

## What to do if she pushes back on a quote later

She might approve a quote in the moment then second-guess it later. If she does:
- Don't argue. Use the next-best alternate from the candidate list.
- Document what she objected to and why — useful for future case studies (Jennifer's preferences will inform how you interview hotel #2, hotel #3).
- Never publish anything she's withdrawn approval on. The Wisconsin partner relationship is more valuable than any single quote.
